Do high gas prices sink the president's party?

Short answer: not reliably.

Rising gas prices are often blamed for midterm losses. Across 12 midterms and 584 state results, the gas price change explains almost none of the swing against the president's party. Presidential approval explains far more.

What it means

Approval carries the signal

Gallup approval before the vote explains about a third of the swing across midterms. Gas prices explain about 3%.

Gas may act through approval

A 10% real gas rise goes with about 1.5 fewer approval points (p ≈ 0.09). Any gas effect probably runs through how voters rate the president.

Watch the bad control

Holding approval fixed, gas turns slightly positive. That is a classic artifact of controlling for a mediator, and it fades once 2022 (the year of the Dobbs ruling) is dropped.

Twelve points is not much

Gas prices move mostly together nationwide, so states differ by only a few points within a year. The data cannot rule out a modest effect. They do rule out a large, consistent one.

Method and sources

  • Swing: change in the president's party's two-party House vote, midterm vs. prior presidential year, counting only districts with both a Democrat and a Republican on the ballot. Source: MIT Election Data + Science Lab, U.S. House 1976–2024.
  • Gas prices: national monthly unleaded regular from the EIA Monthly Energy Review (October values). State annual motor gasoline prices from the EIA State Energy Data System. All prices deflated to 2024 dollars with CPI-U.
  • Controls: CPI inflation and state unemployment (BLS), real per-capita income (BEA), refiner crude oil cost (EIA), Gallup approval before each midterm.
  • Models: OLS with robust standard errors for the 12 national points. State models are weighted by number of districts, with errors clustered by year (pooled) or by state (fixed effects).
